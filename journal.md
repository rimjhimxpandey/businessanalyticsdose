# MSc Business Analytics Journal

A collection of things I wish I knew before and during my MSc in Business Analytics.

The goal isn't to write every detail of my day just one lesson, insight, mistake, or tip that could help someone else.

---

## Day 1 | 24 July 2026

### Topic
You don't need to know everything before starting your Master's.

### What I Learned
When I began my MSc, I thought everyone else would already be experts in Python, SQL, statistics, and machine learning.

The reality was different. Everyone had strengths in different areas. The key was being willing to learn consistently rather than trying to know everything from day one. I learned to focus on the fundamentals first—writing clean code, understanding data structures, and building a good statistical intuition—then layer on more advanced topics as they became necessary for projects or internships. This approach reduced overwhelm and helped me actually retain skills rather than skimming many topics superficially.

### Advice
Start before you feel ready. Progress beats perfection.

## Day 2 | 25 July 2026

### Topic
Your Master's is much more than lectures.

### What I Learned
I initially thought getting good grades would be enough. But over time, I realized that networking events, workshops, certifications, career fairs, volunteering, and LinkedIn all played a huge role in shaping my learning and career trajectory. Lectures provided core knowledge, but the practical skills, industry awareness, and professional connections I gained outside the classroom were equally important. Attending workshops helped me apply theoretical concepts to real-world datasets, while certifications boosted my CV and helped me stand out in job applications. Career fairs and LinkedIn introduced me to employers and alumni whose guidance and referrals led to internships and interviews. Volunteering and student leadership roles developed soft skills like communication, teamwork, and time management that employers value. Over time, I realized that a balanced approach—combining academic performance with extracurricular engagement—was the most effective path to opportunities.

### Advice
Attend opportunities even when they seem optional. Sometimes the biggest career opportunities come from experiences outside the classroom.

## Day 3 | 26 July 2026

### Topic
Say "yes" to opportunities before you think you're ready.

### What I Learned
One of the first things I did after joining the University of Galway was express my interest in becoming a Global Student Ambassador.

At that point, I didn't know exactly where it would lead. I simply knew I wanted to get involved beyond academics and make the most of my Master's journey.

Looking back, that one decision opened doors to meeting students from different countries, representing the university at events, improving my communication skills, and building confidence. It also gave me opportunities to work on projects, lead events, and develop leadership skills that coursework alone wouldn't have provided. The role pushed me to speak publicly, manage logistics, and represent the student body, which improved my confidence and employability.

Sometimes the opportunities that shape your journey aren't part of your course, they're the ones you choose to pursue.

### Advice
Don't wait until your second semester or until you feel "settled." If something genuinely interests you, apply for it. The earlier you step out of your comfort zone, the more rewarding your Master's journey will be—these experiences teach you faster than any lecture and often lead to unexpected networks, mentors, or career directions.

## Day 4 | 27 July 2026

### Topic
Prepare for your part-time job before you actually need one.

### What I Learned
One of the biggest lessons from my journey was that getting a part-time job starts long before your first interview.

As soon as I landed in Ireland, I focused on completing all the essentials—getting my Stamp 2 permission, IRP card, PPS Number, Leap Card, Revenue registration, and everything required to settle legally and practically in a new country. Each document took time, and understanding the application processes ahead of time saved a lot of last-minute stress and allowed me to apply for roles without paperwork delays.

I also prepared an Irish-style CV before applying anywhere.

My job search wasn't limited to one platform. I regularly checked Indeed, visited the career pages of supermarkets and retail stores, joined WhatsApp and Facebook groups, followed local pages on Instagram, and connected with university career services. I reached out to alumni on LinkedIn for advice and monitored notice boards on campus; sometimes local shops advertised vacancies in person, so being proactive in different channels made a real difference.

The biggest thing I learned? Finding a part-time job is often a mix of preparation, consistency, and timing. Luck plays a role, but being ready when an opportunity appears makes all the difference, so keeping documents up to date and applying frequently increases your chances.

### Advice

Don't wait until your savings are running low before you begin.

✔️ Complete your essential documents first.
✔️ Prepare an Irish-style CV.
✔️ Apply through company career pages and job portals.
✔️ Keep an eye on social media and local student groups.
✔️ Don't hesitate to walk into stores and ask if they're hiring.

The more places you look, the more opportunities you'll find.

## Day 5 | 28 July 2026

### Topic
Sometimes your first job isn't your dream job, but it's the one that changes everything.

### What I Learned

When I arrived in Ireland, I had a clear picture of the kind of part-time job I wanted.

Coming from a vegetarian background, I wasn't comfortable working around meat or food preparation. I was mainly looking for roles in bookstores, retail stores, supermarkets, customer service, and positions where handling food was minimal or non-existent.

Over the first few weeks, I applied everywhere. I attended multiple interviews—the first one I remember was at Tesco (Ballybane). Although I wasn't selected, every interview taught me something about how to present myself, answer competency questions, and follow up professionally.

At the same time, I was making the most of university opportunities. I became a Global Student Ambassador, where I helped with campus tours, student surveys, and university events whenever required. These roles provided a flexible schedule and helped me build confidence interacting with prospective students and staff.

I still needed a stable part-time job to support my living expenses.

Towards the end of October 2025, I came across a hiring advertisement for Subway. Honestly, it wasn't the kind of job I had imagined myself doing, but after weeks of searching, I decided to give it a try. I called the number on the advertisement, was invited for an interview, prepared well, and fortunately, everything went smoothly. After successfully completing my trial shifts, I was selected to join the team.

The restaurant in Salthill was a brand-new franchise, so I first received my training at the Newcastle branch before moving to the newly opened store.

Ironically, my first job in Ireland turned out to be in a sandwich shop—the one type of role I had initially hoped to avoid. It was also my first time handling meat. I was nervous at first, but colleagues and training helped me learn food handling, hygiene, and customer service quickly.

Looking back, saying "yes" to an opportunity outside my comfort zone became one of the most important decisions of my journey. The job taught me transferable skills—communication, teamwork, punctuality, problem-solving, and customer empathy—that later helped with applications and interviews in other roles. It also introduced me to a community of colleagues and managers who offered advice and support beyond the workplace.

### Advice

Don't reject an opportunity just because it isn't your ideal job.

Your first job doesn't have to define your career—it simply has to help you take your first step. Every experience teaches you something, builds resilience, and opens doors to new opportunities; the contacts you meet and the references you earn can be as valuable as the skills you gain.

## Day 6 | 29 July 2026

### Topic
Balancing academics, work, and university life isn't easy—but it's possible.

### What I Learned

One of the biggest challenges during my MSc wasn't the coursework itself—it was learning how to balance everything at once.

My course lasted only nine months, which meant assignments, presentations, group projects, and deadlines came one after another. At the same time, I was working part-time at Subway, learning everything from shift routines to customer interactions while trying to keep up with reading, lectures, and assignments.

The first few weeks at Subway were overwhelming. Everything was new—memorising recipes, understanding food safety procedures, serving customers quickly, handling the till, preparing orders during peak times, and learning to communicate clearly under pressure.

Coming from a vegetarian background, I had never handled meat before. Initially, I was hesitant and unsure if I would be able to do the job comfortably. But with training, support from my colleagues, and clear food safety protocols, I soon became confident and efficient.

Outside of work and academics, I also continued my involvement as a Global Student Ambassador whenever events were scheduled and volunteered as a LIFT Ireland Facilitator to strengthen my leadership skills. Juggling these commitments taught me how to manage multiple responsibilities and to prioritize tasks.

There were days when lectures were followed by work, assignments had to be completed late at night, and weekends disappeared into deadlines. Finding time for myself wasn't always easy, but I learned to schedule small breaks, set realistic goals, and ask for help when needed. These strategies helped preserve my wellbeing while staying on top of responsibilities.

Looking back, managing all these responsibilities taught me discipline, resilience, and time management—skills that no classroom alone could have taught me.

### Advice

Don't expect life during your Master's to be perfectly balanced every day.

Some weeks will be focused on assignments.
Some on work.
Some on university events.

Learn to prioritise, stay organised, and remember to take care of yourself along the way. Progress is about consistency, not perfection.

## Day 7 | 30 July 2026

### Topic
Starting over doesn't mean leaving everyone behind.

### What I Learned

One thing I underestimated before moving to Ireland was how much I would miss the people around me.

Back home, I had friends I had grown up with—people who knew me without any explanation. We had shared countless memories, celebrations, late-night conversations, and everyday moments that I had taken for granted; those relationships were a source of comfort and identity.

Moving to a new country meant leaving that comfort behind and starting from zero.

In the beginning, it felt strange. Everyone around me was new, and so was I to them. Building friendships wasn't instant. It happened naturally through lectures, group assignments, university events, part-time work, and shared experiences that slowly created common ground.

Slowly, a few people became more than just classmates or colleagues. They became friends who celebrated small wins, supported each other during assignment deadlines, shared meals after lectures, and explored the city together. These small acts of companionship made the transition feel less lonely.

What I realised is that making friends as an adult is different. It takes effort, time, and being willing to start conversations even when it feels uncomfortable.

The friends I have back home will always be irreplaceable, but I'm grateful that Ireland gave me the chance to build another small circle that made this journey memorable.

### Advice

Stay connected with the people you leave behind, but don't close yourself off to the people you meet along the way.

Sometimes a simple "Hi", joining a group project, or accepting an invitation for coffee can be the beginning of a lifelong friendship. Use tools like video calls, messaging apps, and social media to keep in touch with old friends and to maintain new ones—regular check-ins help sustain relationships across distance.

## Day 8 | 31 July 2026

### Topic
My first paycheck in Ireland wasn't just money—it was independence.

### What I Learned

The day I received my first salary from Subway, I looked at my bank account with a smile that I can't really describe.

It wasn't about earning a huge amount of money. It was about the feeling that I was slowly building a life for myself in a new country.

That paycheck meant I could contribute towards my rent and electricity bills without constantly worrying. It meant buying groceries with my own earnings, treating myself to a coffee after a long shift, and—importantly—feeling like I had some control over my day-to-day finances.

It also gave me the freedom to enjoy the little things that made Ireland feel like home. Visiting the Galway Christmas Market, celebrating Halloween, taking short trips to Dublin and Limerick, exploring the coastline, and joining friends for cultural events helped me feel connected to my new environment.

Of course, there were responsibilities too. Every month I had to think about rent, utility bills, groceries, transport, and making sure there was enough left for essentials. Sometimes that meant choosing between a social outing and saving for bills, which forced me to plan and budget more carefully.

Living independently taught me that financial freedom isn't about spending more—it's about making thoughtful choices.

Looking back, my first paycheck wasn't just a salary.

It was the moment I realised that I was capable of taking care of myself in a country thousands of kilometres away from home.

### Advice

Your first paycheck isn't a reward to spend all at once—it's the beginning of learning how to manage your own life.

Enjoy it. Celebrate it. But also learn to budget, save a little, and spend on experiences that make your journey memorable.

The memories from a weekend trip, a festive evening with friends, or a meal shared after a long week often stay with you much longer than an impulse purchase.
