# MSc Business Analytics Journal

A collection of things I wish I knew before and during my MSc in Business Analytics.

The goal isn't to write every detail of my day—just one lesson, insight, mistake, or tip that could help someone else.

---

## Day 1 | 24 July 2026

### Topic
You don't need to know everything before starting your Master's.

### What I Learned
When I began my MSc, I thought everyone else would already be experts in Python, SQL, statistics, and machine learning.

The reality was different. Everyone had strengths in different areas and gaps in others. Some classmates were confident with coding but struggled with statistics, while others had strong analytical backgrounds but needed more practice with programming. Over time I realised that learning during the course is normal — you don't have to arrive as an expert.

### Advice
Start before you feel ready. Progress beats perfection.

## Day 3 | 26 July 2026

### Topic
Say "yes" to opportunities before you think you're ready.

### What I Learned
One of the first things I did after joining the University of Galway was express my interest in becoming a Global Student Ambassador. At that point, I didn't know exactly where it would lead. I simply applied and said yes to opportunities that came my way.

### Advice

## Day 4 | 27 July 2026

### Topic
Prepare for your part-time job before you actually need one.

### What I Learned
One of the biggest lessons from my journey was that getting a part-time job starts long before your first interview. As soon as I landed in Ireland, I focused on completing all the essential paperwork and understanding local requirements — things like PPS number, bank account, and proof of address.

The biggest takeaway was that readiness matters: luck helps, but preparation and persistence matter more. Being proactive about paperwork and applications reduced stress and allowed me to seize opportunities quickly.

### Advice
Don't wait until your savings are running low before you begin.

✔️ Complete your essential documents first. ✔️ Prepare an Irish-style CV. ✔️ Apply through company career pages and job portals. ✔️ Keep an eye on social media and local student groups for job postings.

The more places you look, the more opportunities you'll find.

## Day 5 | 28 July 2026

### Topic
Sometimes your first job isn't your dream job, but it's the one that changes everything.

### What I Learned
When I arrived in Ireland, I had a clear picture of the kind of part-time job I wanted. Coming from a vegetarian background, I wasn't comfortable working around meat or food preparation and preferred roles that aligned with my values.

At the same time, I continued taking on volunteer roles and university opportunities, which kept my confidence up. Eventually, toward the end of October 2025, I came across a hiring advertisement for a role that I decided to apply for despite my initial reservations.

Despite initial nervousness, the role taught me valuable transferable skills: clear communication with customers and colleagues, teamwork, time management during busy shifts, and resilience in problem-solving.

### Advice
Don't reject an opportunity just because it isn't your ideal job.

Your first job doesn't have to define your career—it simply has to help you take the first step. Every experience teaches you something, builds resilience, and opens doors to new opportunities.

## Day 6 | 29 July 2026

### Topic
Balancing academics, work, and university life isn't easy but it's possible.

### What I Learned
One of the biggest challenges during my MSc wasn't the coursework itself—it was learning how to balance everything at once. My course lasted only nine months, which meant assignments, presentations, exams, and part-time work all needed to fit into a tight schedule.

Those first few weeks were overwhelming, and I sometimes felt exhausted by the end of the day. Coming from a vegetarian background, I had never handled meat before and that added an extra layer of discomfort when working in certain food-service roles.

There were days when lectures were followed by work, assignments had to be completed late at night, and weekends disappeared into deadlines. Over time I learned to prioritise tasks, set realistic daily goals, and communicate with teammates early when group work became challenging.

### Advice
Don't expect life during your Master's to be perfectly balanced every day.

Some weeks will be focused on assignments. Some on work. Some on university events.

Learn to prioritise, stay organised, and remember to take care of yourself along the way. Progress is about consistency, not perfection.

## Day 7 | 30 July 2026

### Topic
Starting over doesn't mean leaving everyone behind.

### What I Learned
One thing I underestimated before moving to Ireland was how much I would miss the people around me. Back home, I had friends I had grown up with—people who knew me without any explanation. We had shared history and easy conversations.

In the beginning it felt strange and sometimes lonely—everyone around me was new, and I was unfamiliar to them. Building friendships took time and often happened through classes, group assignments, and casual invitations.

The friends I have back home remain irreplaceable, but I'm grateful that Ireland gave me the chance to build another small circle that made this journey memorable. Maintaining relationships across distances took effort, but it was possible with regular calls and messages.

### Advice
Stay connected with the people you leave behind, but don't close yourself off to the people you meet along the way.

Sometimes a simple "Hi," joining a group project, or accepting an invitation for coffee can be the beginning of a lifelong friendship. Use tools like video calls, messaging apps, and social media to keep those connections alive.

## Day 9 | 1 August 2026

### Topic
A Master's teaches you more than academics—it teaches you how to work with people.

### What I Learned
When I started my MSc in Business Analytics, I had one goal in mind—to graduate with a First Class Honours (1:1). I knew it wouldn't be easy, especially with a course that lasted only nine months.

The pace was intense. Assignments, projects, presentations, exams, and part-time work all had to fit into the same calendar.

One challenge I didn't expect was group assignments.

Within a very short time after joining the university, we had to form groups of four or five students for almost every module. Since we barely knew each other, most groups were formed randomly.

Although we were all from India, we came from different states, universities, educational backgrounds, and ways of thinking. Everyone had different priorities, working styles, and expectations. Our differences sometimes led to friction, but we learned to communicate and find compromises.

There were times when I felt not everyone was contributing equally. Instead of letting that affect the quality of the work, I chose to focus on what I could control. If I felt the team was giving 50%, I would ensure my contribution was consistent and constructive.

That didn't mean doing everything alone. It meant communicating, negotiating, dividing responsibilities, resolving conflicts, and making sure we submitted work that we were all proud of.

Those experiences taught me that teamwork isn't about finding perfect teammates—it's about learning how to work with different personalities while keeping the end goal in mind.

Looking back, earning my 1:1 wasn't just about studying hard. It was about staying consistent, taking responsibility, and learning how to collaborate under pressure.

### Advice
Not every group project will be smooth, and not every teammate will work the way you do.

Focus on communication, mutual understanding, and delivering your best. Your attitude towards teamwork will often matter just as much as your technical skills.

## Day 9 (Continuation) | 1 August 2026

### Topic
Just when I thought the hardest part was over, Semester 2 proved me wrong.

### What I Learned

Finishing Semester 1 with First Class Honours felt like a huge achievement. It reassured me that all the late nights, assignments, balancing work at Subway, and university commitments had been worth it.

But there was one problem.

Getting a 1:1 in Semester 1 wasn't enough—I had to maintain it.

Since I was close to the grade boundary, every assignment, presentation, and exam in Semester 2 suddenly felt even more important. The biggest challenge was still ahead of me: a 30-credit Business Analytics project that would demand sustained effort and focus.

By then, I had realised my Semester 1 project group wasn't the right fit. So, for Semester 2, I decided to step out of my comfort zone and form a new team. Fortunately, the new group started on a much better footing and we were able to manage expectations more clearly.

At the same time, the academic workload became much more technical.

Programming modules demanded a different level of effort. Although I came from an engineering background, Python for Business Analytics was completely new to me. There were moments when I questioned whether I could keep up, but consistent practice and asking for help made a big difference.

Outside academics, I didn't want to miss opportunities that could help my career. Alongside my studies, I enrolled in the University Employability Award, attended career fairs in both semesters, and applied for internships and part-time roles that aligned with my goals.

Little did I know that Semester 2 was about to test me in ways I hadn't imagined. The challenges would be deeper, the competition tougher, and my resolve would be pushed to its limits. But they would also teach me resilience and adaptability.

### Advice

Sometimes success creates new pressure.

Celebrate your achievements, but don't let them make you complacent. Every semester, every project, and every opportunity is a fresh start.

## Day 10 | 2 August 2026

### Topic
Christmas break wasn't really a break.

### What I Learned

For many students, the Christmas holidays meant going home, relaxing, and taking a break from university.

For me, it looked a little different.

Since classes were on a break, I increased my working hours at Subway to 40 hours a week. It was the perfect opportunity to earn more, support myself financially, and prepare for the months ahead. I scheduled shifts around assignment deadlines and used quieter periods to get ahead on coursework.

But even though lectures had paused, university hadn't.

Assignments still had deadlines. Group discussions continued. Semester 2 planning had already begun. My goal of graduating with a First Class Honours (1:1) never left my mind.

Some days started with work, ended with assignments, and somewhere in between I'd squeeze in grocery shopping, cooking, laundry, and trying to call home. The rhythm was relentless, but it was also my reality.

Despite the busy schedule, Christmas in Galway became one of my favourite memories. Walking through the Christmas Market, spending time with friends, trying different cafés, and seeing the city decorated for the season all helped create a sense of belonging.

Those small moments helped me recharge before another demanding semester.

I also realised something important during this period.

Balancing life doesn't always mean having free time. Sometimes it simply means making room for the people and moments that make all the hard work worthwhile.

Looking back, Christmas wasn't a break from responsibility.

It was a lesson in managing responsibility while still making memories.

### Advice

Work hard when you have the opportunity, but don't forget to enjoy the little moments along the way.

The degree, the job, and the money all matter—but so do the memories you create while chasing them.

## Day 11 | 3 August 2026

### Topic
Sometimes life gives you a second chance.

### What I Learned

One of the very first interviews I attended after arriving in Ireland was for Tesco. I remember preparing with hope, imagining myself working there. Unfortunately, I wasn't selected.

At the time, it was disappointing, but looking back, I realise I simply wasn't ready yet.

A few weeks later, I started my first part-time job in Ireland at Subway. It wasn't the role I had imagined for myself, but it gave me something much more valuable than a paycheck—it gave me confidence, routine, and experience working in a team.

For months, I balanced university, assignments, leadership roles, and work at Subway. There were challenging days, especially during busy shifts, when I had to manage almost everything myself. Those experiences taught me time management and resilience.

On **19th June**, I completed my final shift at Subway. Walking out after my last day felt emotional because it was the place that had welcomed me when I was just beginning my journey in Ireland.

The next day—**20th June**, which also happened to be my birthday—I started a new chapter as a Customer Assistant at Tesco.

It felt like life had come full circle.

Everything was new again. New colleagues, new responsibilities, new systems, and another learning curve. But this time, I walked in with confidence because of everything Subway had already taught me.

One thing I immediately appreciated was the workplace culture. I noticed how much importance was given to teamwork, respect, and work-life balance. If my shift finished at 10:00 p.m., I finished on time and didn't feel pressured to stay longer without notice.

I'm currently completing my probation period. Clearing my first probation review and receiving my first green card was a small but meaningful milestone. I still have more reviews ahead, but every shift is another chance to learn.

Looking back, I'm grateful for both jobs.

Subway gave me my first opportunity.

Tesco gave me the opportunity I had hoped for when I first arrived.

### Advice

Don't be discouraged if your first interview doesn't go the way you expected.

Sometimes the role you don't get prepares you for the one you're truly meant to have. Every experience builds skills that will eventually open the right door.

## Day 12 | 4 August 2026

### Topic
Your Master's isn't just a degree—it's a transformation.

### What I Learned

As I reflect on my journey through the MSc in Business Analytics at the University of Galway, I realize that the transformation I've undergone extends far beyond academic knowledge and technical skill.

Today, as I approach the end of my Master's, I'm a different person.

Not just in terms of what I know—Python, SQL, statistics, machine learning, and business analytics frameworks are now part of my toolkit. But in terms of who I've become: someone more confident, more curious, and more willing to take on challenges.

Throughout this journey, I've learned that a Master's degree is not just about the classroom experience. It's about every conversation with a professor, every group project that pushed me to understand different perspectives, and every extracurricular role that taught me leadership.

I've learned that the real value of a Master's lies in the people you meet and the relationships you build. My classmates, who came from different parts of India and the world, taught me as much as any lecture.

I've also learned that a Master's teaches you about yourself—your strengths, your weaknesses, your limits, and your untapped potential. There were moments during the program when I questioned whether I could keep up, but those moments also showed me where I could grow.

Beyond academics, this Master's has given me independence and self-reliance. I've lived in a new country, managed my own finances, worked multiple jobs, faced rejection and setback, and learned to pick myself up and keep going.

My time in Ireland has also expanded my perspective on what's possible. I've attended career fairs and networking events that opened doors I didn't know existed. I've made friends from different cultures and learned to appreciate new ways of thinking.

### Advice

A Master's degree is not a finish line—it's a turning point.

The knowledge you gain is valuable, but the person you become matters more. Don't just focus on grades; focus on learning, growing, and pushing yourself beyond what you thought was possible. Take opportunities outside the classroom and invest in building skills that will help you long after graduation.

Most importantly, recognize that your Master's experience is uniquely yours. There's no "perfect" way to do it. Some people will balance everything perfectly; others will struggle. Some will graduate with high honours; others will learn lessons that shape their future careers.

As I prepare to move into the next chapter of my career, I carry with me not just a degree, but also the confidence that comes from knowing I can handle challenges, the humility that comes from working alongside others, and the curiosity that keeps me learning.

## Day 13 | 05 August 2026

### Topic
The loneliness nobody talks about as an international student.

### What I Learned

When people think about studying abroad, they often picture beautiful campuses, weekend trips, new friendships, and exciting opportunities.

And yes, all of that is real.

But there's another side that people don't always talk about.

Loneliness.

Not because you don't have friends—but because no matter how amazing the people around you are, there are moments when you miss the comfort of home.

I was fortunate to make some wonderful friends in Ireland. We explored Galway together, visited the Christmas Market, celebrated Halloween, planned trips to Dublin and Limerick, grabbed food after lectures, and laughed through stressful assignment weeks.

Yet there were evenings when I walked back to my room after work, cooked dinner alone, and wished I could simply sit with my family without checking the time difference.

There were days when I achieved something I was proud of—whether it was a good grade, a new opportunity, or even getting my job at Tesco—and realised that celebrating through a phone call isn't quite the same as having your loved ones beside you.

Being an international student teaches you independence, but it also teaches you the value of the relationships you sometimes take for granted.

Over time, I stopped looking at loneliness as something negative.

Instead, I started seeing it as a reminder of how deeply I love the people back home and how fortunate I am to slowly build another family here through friends, classmates, and colleagues.

You don't stop missing home.

You simply learn to carry a little piece of it with you wherever you go.

### Advice

If you're moving abroad, don't expect yourself to feel happy every single day.

Stay connected with your family. Make time for friends. Say yes to plans, even when you're tired. And if you're having a difficult day, remember that you're not the only one feeling this way.

Almost every international student has moments of loneliness—they just don't always talk about them.
