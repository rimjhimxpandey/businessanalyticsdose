# MSc Business Analytics Journal

A collection of things I wish I knew before and during my MSc in Business Analytics.

The goal isn't to write every detail of my day—just one lesson, insight, mistake, or tip that could help someone else.

---

## Day 1 | 24 July 2026

### Topic
You don't need to know everything before starting your Master's.

### What I Learned
When I began my MSc, I thought everyone else would already be experts in Python, SQL, statistics, and machine learning.

The reality was different. Everyone had strengths in different areas and gaps in others. Some classmates were confident with coding but struggled with statistics, while others had strong analytical foundations but needed to develop their programming skills. The best part? Everyone was willing to learn, and no one expected perfection on day one.

### Advice
Start before you feel ready. Progress beats perfection.

## Day 3 | 26 July 2026

### Topic
Say "yes" to opportunities before you think you're ready.

### What I Learned
One of the first things I did after joining the University of Galway was express my interest in becoming a Global Student Ambassador. At that point, I didn't know exactly where it would lead. I simply saw an opportunity to step outside my comfort zone and decided to apply, even though I wasn't certain I was "ready."

Sometimes the opportunities that shape your journey aren't part of your course; they are the extracurricular experiences you choose to pursue. Taking a role like this doesn't require perfection—just the willingness to try, learn, and grow. The Global Student Ambassador program gave me visibility on campus, confidence in speaking to new students and families, and connections with people I wouldn't have met otherwise.

### Advice
Don't wait until your second semester or until you feel "settled." If something genuinely interests you, apply for it. The earlier you step out of your comfort zone, the more rewarding your Master's journey will be.

## Day 4 | 27 July 2026

### Topic
Prepare for your part-time job before you actually need one.

### What I Learned
One of the biggest lessons from my journey was that getting a part-time job starts long before your first interview. As soon as I landed in Ireland, I focused on completing all the essential paperwork—PPS number, bank account, visa documentation—so that when opportunities came, I was ready to act immediately. I didn't wait until my savings were depleted to start searching. Instead, I prepared my CV, researched companies, and familiarized myself with Irish job markets while I still had time and energy.

The biggest takeaway was that readiness matters: luck helps, but preparation and persistence matter more. Being proactive about paperwork and applications reduced stress and allowed me to seize opportunities without panic. Every application I sent, every career fair I attended, and every networking conversation brought me closer to my first job.

### Advice
Don't wait until your savings are running low before you begin.

✔️ Complete your essential documents first. ✔️ Prepare an Irish-style CV. ✔️ Apply through company career pages and job portals. ✔️ Keep an eye on social media and local student groups.

The more places you look, the more opportunities you'll find.

## Day 5 | 28 July 2026

### Topic
Sometimes your first job isn't your dream job, but it's the one that changes everything.

### What I Learned
When I arrived in Ireland, I had a clear picture of the kind of part-time job I wanted. Coming from a vegetarian background, I wasn't comfortable working around meat or food preparation and preferred something in a corporate or tech environment. I had imagined landing an analytics or business role aligned with my field of study.

At the same time, I continued taking on volunteer roles and university opportunities, which kept my confidence up. Eventually, toward the end of October 2025, I came across a hiring advertisement for Subway. My first reaction wasn't excitement—it was hesitation. This wasn't the job I had imagined. It involved food preparation, which conflicted with my comfort zone. But I also realized that I couldn't afford to be too selective. I applied, interviewed, and was offered the position.

Despite initial nervousness, the role taught me valuable transferable skills: clear communication with customers and colleagues, teamwork, time management during busy shifts, and resilience in problem-solving. More importantly, it gave me the confidence to know I could handle a job while managing my Master's coursework. Every shift taught me something new about myself and what I was capable of.

### Advice
Don't reject an opportunity just because it isn't your ideal job.

Your first job doesn't have to define your career—it simply has to help you take the first step. Every experience teaches you something, builds resilience, and opens doors to new opportunities.

## Day 6 | 29 July 2026

### Topic
Balancing academics, work, and university life isn't easy but it's possible.

### What I Learned
One of the biggest challenges during my MSc wasn't the coursework itself—it was learning how to balance everything at once. My course lasted only nine months, which meant assignments, presentations, group projects, and exams were packed tightly into the calendar. Add a part-time job on top of that, and the pressure multiplied quickly.

Those first few weeks were overwhelming, and I sometimes felt exhausted by the end of the day. Coming from a vegetarian background, I had never handled meat before and that added an extra layer of discomfort to my new job. I remember nights when I would finish a shift at 10 p.m., come home, and still need to work on assignments or prepare for presentations. But over time, I stopped seeing these challenges as separate problems and started viewing them as interconnected parts of my life that needed strategic management.

There were days when lectures were followed by work, assignments had to be completed late at night, and weekends disappeared into deadlines. Over time I learned to prioritise tasks, set realistic daily goals, and most importantly, accept that some days would be better than others. On good days, I could accomplish lectures, work, and assignments. On harder days, I focused on just getting through one thing at a time.

### Advice
Don't expect life during your Master's to be perfectly balanced every day.

Some weeks will be focused on assignments. Some on work. Some on university events.

Learn to prioritise, stay organised, and remember to take care of yourself along the way. Progress is about consistency, not perfection.

## Day 7 | 30 July 2026

### Topic
Starting over doesn't mean leaving everyone behind.

### What I Learned
One thing I underestimated before moving to Ireland was how much I would miss the people around me. Back home, I had friends I had grown up with—people who knew me without any explanation. We had shared history, inside jokes, and a comfort that only comes from years of knowing each other. Moving to another country meant leaving all of that behind.

In the beginning it felt strange and sometimes lonely—everyone around me was new, and I was unfamiliar to them. Building friendships took time and often happened through classes, group assignments, and university events. I had to actively put myself out there, initiate conversations, and say yes to social invitations even when I was tired or anxious.

The friends I have back home remain irreplaceable, but I'm grateful that Ireland gave me the chance to build another small circle that made this journey memorable. Maintaining relationships across distances required effort—scheduled video calls, messaging when I could, sharing updates about my life—but it was worth it. Over time, I realized that distance doesn't diminish old friendships if you invest in them, and it doesn't prevent new ones from forming if you open yourself to the possibility.

### Advice
Stay connected with the people you leave behind, but don't close yourself off to the people you meet along the way.

Sometimes a simple "Hi," joining a group project, or accepting an invitation for coffee can be the beginning of a lifelong friendship. Use tools like video calls, messaging apps, and social media to keep old bonds strong while building new ones. Both are possible.

## Day 9 | 1 August 2026

### Topic
A Master's teaches you more than academics—it teaches you how to work with people.

### What I Learned
When I started my MSc in Business Analytics, I had one goal in mind—to graduate with a First Class Honours (1:1). I knew it wouldn't be easy, especially with a course that lasted only nine months.

The pace was intense. Assignments, projects, presentations, exams, and part-time work all had to fit into the same calendar.

One challenge I didn't expect was group assignments.

Within a very short time after joining the university, we had to form groups of four or five students for almost every module. Since we barely knew each other, most groups were formed randomly.

Although we were all from India, we came from different states, universities, educational backgrounds, and ways of thinking. Everyone had different priorities, working styles, and expectations. Our very first group project felt like we were learning how to communicate while simultaneously trying to produce quality work. Some team members were detail-oriented; others preferred to think big picture. Some were organized planners; others preferred spontaneous work sessions.

There were times when I felt not everyone was contributing equally. Instead of letting that affect the quality of the work, I chose to focus on what I could control. If I felt the team was giving 50%, I would put in 70% to make sure we reached 100%. But I also learned to speak up when workload was unbalanced and to address it respectfully with teammates.

That didn't mean doing everything alone. It meant communicating, negotiating, dividing responsibilities, resolving conflicts, and making sure we submitted work that we were all proud of.

Those experiences taught me that teamwork isn't about finding perfect teammates—it's about learning how to work with different personalities while keeping the end goal in mind.

Looking back, earning my 1:1 wasn't just about studying hard. It was about staying consistent, taking responsibility, and learning how to collaborate under pressure.

### Advice
Not every group project will be smooth, and not every teammate will work the way you do.

Focus on communication, mutual understanding, and delivering your best. Your attitude towards teamwork will often matter just as much as your technical skills.

## Day 9 (Continuation) | 1 August 2026

### Topic
Just when I thought the hardest part was over, Semester 2 proved me wrong.

### What I Learned

Finishing Semester 1 with First Class Honours felt like a huge achievement. It reassured me that all the late nights, assignments, balancing work at Subway, and university commitments had been worth it. I had proven to myself that I could manage everything simultaneously. I was proud, relieved, and ready to move into Semester 2 with momentum.

But there was one problem.

Getting a 1:1 in Semester 1 wasn't enough—I had to maintain it.

Since I was close to the grade boundary, every assignment, presentation, and exam in Semester 2 suddenly felt even more important. The biggest challenge was still ahead of me: a 30-credit Business Analytics project that would span the entire semester. This project required independent research, data analysis, presentations, and a final report. It was the most significant piece of work in the entire program.

By then, I had realised my Semester 1 project group wasn't the right fit. So, for Semester 2, I decided to step out of my comfort zone and form a new team. Fortunately, the new group started on a much stronger footing—we communicated better, divided work more fairly, and had aligned expectations from day one. Having the right team made tackling the 30-credit project feel more manageable.

At the same time, the academic workload became much more technical.

Programming modules demanded a different level of effort. Although I came from an engineering background, Python for Business Analytics was completely new to me. There were moments when I questioned whether I could actually master the language while managing everything else. But instead of giving up, I started allocating specific time blocks for coding practice and sought help from classmates and online resources whenever I got stuck.

Outside academics, I didn't want to miss opportunities that could help my career. Alongside my studies, I enrolled in the University Employability Award, attended career fairs in both semesters, and applied for internships and graduate roles that interested me. I knew that the job market was competitive, and I needed to build my resume and network while still in university.

Little did I know that Semester 2 was about to test me in ways I hadn't imagined. The challenges would be deeper, the competition tougher, and my resolve would be pushed to its limits. But they would also teach me the most valuable lessons about resilience, self-belief, and what I was truly capable of achieving.

### Advice

Sometimes success creates new pressure.

Celebrate your achievements, but don't let them make you complacent. Every semester, every project, and every opportunity is a fresh start.

## Day 10 | 2 August 2026

### Topic
Christmas break wasn't really a break.

### What I Learned

For many students, the Christmas holidays meant going home, relaxing, and taking a break from university.

For me, it looked a little different.

Since classes were on a break, I increased my working hours at Subway to 40 hours a week. It was the perfect opportunity to earn more, support myself financially, and prepare for the months ahead. I saw the break not as a time to stop working, but as a time to shift my focus toward earning while knowing that classes had paused.

But even though lectures had paused, university hadn't.

Assignments still had deadlines. Group discussions continued. Semester 2 planning had already begun. My goal of graduating with a First Class Honours (1:1) never left my mind.

Some days started with work, ended with assignments, and somewhere in between I'd squeeze in grocery shopping, cooking, laundry, and trying to call home. The rhythm was relentless, but it was also my new normal. I had learned to move through it without overthinking every moment.

Despite the busy schedule, Christmas in Galway became one of my favourite memories. Walking through the Christmas Market, spending time with friends, trying different cafés, and seeing the city decorated with lights reminded me that there was beauty in the midst of the hustle. These moments became my anchor points—the reasons I was pushing through the difficult times.

Those small moments helped me recharge before another demanding semester.

I also realised something important during this period.

Balancing life doesn't always mean having free time. Sometimes it simply means making room for the people and moments that make all the hard work worthwhile.

Looking back, Christmas wasn't a break from responsibility.

It was a lesson in managing responsibility while still making memories.

### Advice

Work hard when you have the opportunity, but don't forget to enjoy the little moments along the way.

The degree, the job, and the money all matter—but so do the memories you create while chasing them.

## Day 11 | 3 August 2026

### Topic
Sometimes life gives you a second chance.

### What I Learned

One of the very first interviews I attended after arriving in Ireland was for Tesco. I remember preparing with hope, imagining myself working there. Unfortunately, I wasn't selected.

At the time, it was disappointing, but looking back, I realise I simply wasn't ready yet.

A few weeks later, I started my first part-time job in Ireland at Subway. It wasn't the role I had imagined for myself, but it gave me something much more valuable than a paycheck—it gave me confidence, customer service experience, resilience, and the ability to adapt to a completely new environment.

For months, I balanced university, assignments, leadership roles, and work at Subway. There were challenging days, especially during busy shifts, when I had to manage almost everything myself. Those experiences pushed me out of my comfort zone and taught me to stay calm under pressure.

On **19th June**, I completed my final shift at Subway. Walking out after my last day felt emotional because it was the place that had welcomed me when I was just beginning my journey in Ireland.

The next day—**20th June**, which also happened to be my birthday—I started a new chapter as a Customer Assistant at Tesco.

It felt like life had come full circle.

Everything was new again. New colleagues, new responsibilities, new systems, and another learning curve. But this time, I walked in with confidence because of everything Subway had already taught me.

One thing I immediately appreciated was the workplace culture. I noticed how much importance was given to teamwork, respect, and work-life balance. If my shift finished at 10:00 p.m., I finished at 10:00 p.m.—not five or ten minutes later. It showed me how much they valued employees' time.

I'm currently completing my probation period. Clearing my first probation review and receiving my first green card was a small but meaningful milestone. I still have more reviews ahead, but every shift is another opportunity to learn, improve, and prove myself.

Looking back, I'm grateful for both jobs.

Subway gave me my first opportunity.

Tesco gave me the opportunity I had hoped for when I first arrived.

### Advice

Don't be discouraged if your first interview doesn't go the way you expected.

Sometimes the role you don't get prepares you for the one you're truly meant to have. Every experience builds skills that will eventually open the right door.

## Day 12 | 4 August 2026

### Topic
Your Master's isn't just a degree—it's a transformation.

### What I Learned

As I reflect on my journey through the MSc in Business Analytics at the University of Galway, I realize that the transformation I've undergone extends far beyond academic knowledge and technical skills. When I first arrived in Ireland nine months ago, I was uncertain, cautious, and afraid of making mistakes. I worried about whether I belonged, whether I was smart enough, and whether I could survive in a country I had never lived in before.

Today, as I approach the end of my Master's, I'm a different person.

Not just in terms of what I know—Python, SQL, statistics, machine learning, and business analytics frameworks are now part of my toolkit. But in terms of who I've become: someone more confident, more resilient, and more willing to embrace uncertainty as a catalyst for growth.

Throughout this journey, I've learned that a Master's degree is not just about the classroom experience. It's about every conversation with a professor, every group project that pushed me to understand different perspectives, every job shift that tested my patience and problem-solving abilities, and every small decision to say "yes" to opportunities that scared me.

I've learned that the real value of a Master's lies in the people you meet and the relationships you build. My classmates, who came from different parts of India and the world, taught me as much as any lecture did. They taught me how to collaborate under pressure, how to respect diverse viewpoints, and how to push through challenges together. Some of these people have become friends I know I'll stay in touch with long after graduation.

I've also learned that a Master's teaches you about yourself—your strengths, your weaknesses, your limits, and your untapped potential. There were moments during the program when I questioned whether I was good enough, especially during Semester 2 when the workload became more technical and demanding. But each time I doubted myself, I pushed through, and each time I came out stronger.

Beyond academics, this Master's has given me independence and self-reliance. I've lived in a new country, managed my own finances, worked multiple jobs, faced rejection and setback, and learned to pick myself up and try again. These life lessons matter just as much as the degree itself.

My time in Ireland has also expanded my perspective on what's possible. I've attended career fairs and networking events that opened doors I didn't know existed. I've made friends from different cultures and backgrounds. I've explored a beautiful country and experienced traditions very different from my own. I've celebrated achievements and learned from failures. All of these experiences have shaped how I see myself and what I believe I can accomplish.

### Advice

A Master's degree is not a finish line—it's a turning point.

The knowledge you gain is valuable, but the person you become matters more. Don't just focus on grades; focus on learning, growing, and pushing yourself beyond what you thought was possible. Take opportunities that scare you. Build genuine relationships. Make memories. Support your classmates. And remember that everyone around you is figuring it out as they go, just like you are.

Most importantly, recognize that your Master's experience is uniquely yours. There's no "perfect" way to do it. Some people will balance everything perfectly; others will struggle. Some will graduate with first-class honors; others will graduate with different grades. But if you've shown up, tried your best, and grown as a person, you've succeeded.

As I prepare to move into the next chapter of my career, I carry with me not just a degree, but also the confidence that comes from knowing I can handle challenges, the humility that comes from working with brilliant people, and the gratitude that comes from recognizing how far I've come.
